Revenue and tax compliance

Can a nonprofit charge fees or sell products?

Yes. A nonprofit can earn revenue and may finish a year with more income than expenses. The important questions are how the activity advances its exempt purpose, whether any unrelated business is regularly carried on, and where the money goes.

The short answer: “Nonprofit” does not mean “no revenue” or “no surplus.” Fees and sales are allowed, but tax exemption is tied to charitable operation—not to giving every product or service away.

Start with the reason for the activity

A fee or sale can directly carry out the mission. Examples might include an educational nonprofit charging affordable tuition for its classes, a museum selling admission, or a workforce program selling goods made as part of job training. The facts matter: simply using the profit for a good cause does not by itself make an unrelated commercial activity substantially related.

The IRS generally treats an activity as an unrelated business when all three conditions apply:

  1. It is a trade or business. The activity involves selling goods or performing services to produce income.
  2. It is regularly carried on. Its frequency and continuity resemble how a taxable business would conduct a similar activity.
  3. It is not substantially related to the exempt purpose. The activity itself does not contribute importantly to accomplishing that purpose, apart from producing money.

Three common revenue patterns

PatternGeneral federal tax direction
Mission-related fees

A volunteer-run bake sale or a thrift store selling substantially all donated goods		May fall within a statutory exception even if the activity otherwise looks commercial.
Regular unrelated sales
An ongoing business that does not itself advance the exempt purpose		May generate unrelated business income and require Form 990-T; a substantial unrelated operation can raise broader exemption concerns.

These are screening examples, not automatic classifications. Advertising, sponsorships, rentals, royalties, memberships, and online sales can each require a more specific analysis.

When Form 990-T enters the picture

The 2025 IRS Form 990-T instructions say an exempt organization generally must file Form 990-T when it has $1,000 or more in gross income from a regularly conducted unrelated trade or business. Gross income is not the same as net profit. Filing Form 990-T is also separate from filing the organization's regular Form 990, Form 990-EZ, or Form 990-N.

Having unrelated business income does not automatically destroy exemption. The IRS can tax that income. But if activities serving nonexempt purposes become substantial compared with the organization's exempt work, the issue can extend beyond the tax bill to whether the organization still satisfies the operational test.

Important exceptions: The IRS lists exceptions for businesses where substantially all work is performed by volunteers, activities operated primarily for the convenience of members, students, patients, officers, or employees, and sales of merchandise substantially all received as gifts or contributions. Other modifications and exclusions can apply too.

Keep the money serving the mission

A 501(c)(3) cannot distribute net earnings to founders, directors, or other private individuals. It can pay reasonable compensation for real work and ordinary business expenses, but insiders should not receive excessive pay, special profit distributions, or unfair deals. Use independent approval, appropriate comparison information, a written conflict-of-interest process, and complete board minutes.

A practical checklist before launching sales

  • Write down how the product or service contributes to the nonprofit's stated exempt purpose.
  • Prepare a realistic budget and keep related and potentially unrelated revenue and expenses identifiable in the books.
  • Have the board approve the activity and document any insider compensation or contracts.
  • Check whether the activity changes projections reported in a pending or approved exemption application.
  • Review the current Form 990-T threshold and exceptions with a qualified tax professional.
  • Check state and local sales-tax, licensing, registration, and charitable-solicitation rules separately.
  • Continue the organization's normal annual IRS reporting.

Federal exemption and state sales tax are different

IRS recognition under section 501(c)(3) concerns federal income tax. It does not automatically exempt every purchase or sale from state sales tax. States define their own exemptions, taxable products and services, registration requirements, and collection rules. Check the revenue department for each state where the nonprofit does business.

Frequently asked questions

Can a nonprofit charge fees for its services?

Yes. A nonprofit may charge program fees. The key federal tax questions are whether the activity furthers the exempt purpose, whether any unrelated business activity is regularly carried on, and whether earnings improperly benefit private individuals.

Can a nonprofit sell products and still be tax-exempt?

Yes. Product sales do not automatically end tax-exempt status. Sales that substantially further the exempt purpose may be related activity; regularly conducted sales that are not substantially related may generate unrelated business income, subject to exceptions and exclusions.

When does a nonprofit file Form 990-T?

Under the current IRS instructions, an exempt organization generally files Form 990-T if it has $1,000 or more of gross income from a regularly conducted unrelated trade or business. This is a gross-income filing threshold, not a net-profit threshold.

Are nonprofit sales automatically exempt from sales tax?

No. Federal income-tax exemption does not automatically decide state sales-tax obligations. Check the rules in every state where the organization sells taxable goods or services.
