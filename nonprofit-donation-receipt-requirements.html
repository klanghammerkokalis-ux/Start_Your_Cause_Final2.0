Donor records

What must a nonprofit donation receipt include?

For a single contribution of $250 or more, the donor needs a timely written acknowledgment containing specific information. A good new-nonprofit practice is to acknowledge every gift, while treating the $250 acknowledgment and $75 quid-pro-quo rules separately.

Use the receipt checklist →
The core rule: Identify the organization and contribution, then say whether the donor received anything in return. For donated property, describe the property but do not assign its value.

Donation acknowledgment checklist

For a single cash or noncash contribution valued at $250 or more, include:

  • The nonprofit's legal name.
  • The cash amount for a monetary gift.
  • A description—but not the value—of donated property.
  • A no-benefit statement if the organization provided no goods or services in return.
  • A description and good-faith value estimate if the donor received goods or services.
  • The required religious-benefit statement when the only return benefit was an intangible religious benefit.

The IRS does not prescribe a special acknowledgment form. A letter, receipt, postcard, computer-generated form, or email can work if it contains the required information.

Two thresholds founders often confuse

ThresholdWhat it generally means
$250 or moreFor each single contribution, the donor needs a contemporaneous written acknowledgment to substantiate a federal charitable deduction. Separate gifts under $250 generally are not combined solely to reach this threshold.
More than $75If the payment is partly a donation and partly for goods or services, the charity generally must provide a quid-pro-quo disclosure—even if the deductible portion is $75 or less.

For monetary gifts of any amount, the donor must keep an appropriate bank record or written communication from the organization. Sending receipts for every gift is therefore a useful operating practice even when the $250 acknowledgment rule does not apply.

Sample wording when nothing was provided in return

Sample: Thank you for your contribution of $[amount], received on [date], to [legal organization name]. No goods or services were provided in exchange for this contribution.

Add the donor's name, the organization's contact information, and an internal receipt or transaction number as practical recordkeeping details. The IRS-required content—not decorative language—is what matters for federal substantiation.

Sample wording when the donor received something

Sample: Thank you for your payment of $[amount], received on [date], to [legal organization name]. In return, you received [description of goods or services], which we estimate had a fair market value of $[value]. The amount potentially deductible for federal income-tax purposes is limited to the excess of your payment over the value of the goods or services received.

Do not call the entire ticket price or event payment a donation when the donor received a meal, admission, merchandise, or another substantial benefit. The organization must make a good-faith estimate of the return benefit where the rule applies.

When must the donor receive it?

For a contemporaneous acknowledgment, the donor must receive it by the earlier of the date the donor files the original federal return for the contribution year or that return's due date, including extensions. IRS Publication 1771 notes that charities typically send acknowledgments by January 31 following the donation year.

An annual summary may acknowledge several contributions of $250 or more, provided it contains the required information for those gifts. The donor keeps the acknowledgment with tax records rather than attaching it to the individual return.

Special handling for property and pending organizations

  • Noncash gifts: Describe the donated property, but do not state its fair market value. Additional donor requirements can apply, including Form 8283 and, for certain gifts, a qualified appraisal.
  • Volunteer services: The value of a person's time is generally not treated the same as a cash or property contribution. Unreimbursed expenses may follow separate rules.
  • Pending exemption: A receipt should not promise deductibility merely because an organization has incorporated or applied to the IRS. Explain the organization's actual status accurately and review the IRS rules governing contributions while an exemption application is pending.
  • State obligations: Donation acknowledgments do not replace charitable-solicitation registration, state disclosures, gaming rules, sales-tax requirements, or other fundraising obligations.

Frequently asked questions

What must a receipt include for a gift of $250 or more?

Identify the organization; state the cash amount or describe donated property without valuing it; and state whether the organization provided goods or services. If it did, describe them and give a good-faith value estimate.

Can the nonprofit email the acknowledgment?

Yes. IRS Publication 1771 says an acknowledgment may be provided electronically, including by email addressed to the donor.

Should the nonprofit value donated property?

No. Describe the property, but do not include its fair market value in the acknowledgment. Valuation is generally the donor's responsibility.

Does a receipt automatically make the gift deductible?

No. Deductibility depends on the recipient organization's eligibility, the gift, any benefit received, and the donor's circumstances. The receipt documents facts; it does not create tax-exempt status or guarantee a deduction.
