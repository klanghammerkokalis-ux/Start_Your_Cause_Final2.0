Guided DIY or legal help

Do I need a lawyer to start a nonprofit?

Not for every straightforward formation. Current IRS instructions allow an authorized organizational official—not necessarily an attorney—to sign Form 1023. But individualized legal review can be valuable when the structure, activities, relationships, or governing documents are complicated.

The short answer: Federal Form 1023 instructions do not require a lawyer. State rules vary. In Illinois, the Secretary of State's instructions do not say an attorney must be the incorporator or submit the Articles, but the agency strongly recommends legal review because a particular nonprofit may need additional provisions.

What the IRS actually requires

Form 1023 is filed electronically through Pay.gov. The application must be digitally signed by an officer, director, trustee, or other official authorized to sign for the organization. The signer must take responsibility for the accuracy of the application under penalties of perjury.

An organization may choose professional representation. If it wants an attorney, CPA, enrolled agent, or other eligible person to represent it before the IRS, it generally uploads Form 2848, Power of Attorney and Declaration of Representative. That representative still cannot sign Form 1023 unless the person is also an authorized organizational official.

This distinction matters: professional help is optional in the federal application process, but the organization itself remains responsible for complete and accurate information.

What Illinois says

Illinois requires the incorporator to be a natural person age 18 or older. Its online nonprofit-formation instructions do not require that person to be an attorney. The same instructions warn that Articles may need additional provisions for the organization's situation and strongly recommend that legal counsel review documents before submission.

The Secretary of State is an administrative filing agency and cannot provide legal, accounting, or tax advice. Acceptance of Articles therefore does not mean the document contains every provision needed for federal tax exemption, fundraising, governance, or a specialized activity.

When guided DIY may be reasonable

A founder may be more comfortable preparing materials without individualized counsel when the organization has:

  • A conventional nonprofit-corporation structure and a clear charitable purpose.
  • A functioning board with no unusual control or insider arrangements.
  • Standard programs, funding sources, and contracts.
  • No transfer of an existing business, major property, intellectual property, or liabilities.
  • The time and confidence to verify current state and IRS instructions and submit filings directly.

Even in a straightforward case, the board should read every document, keep consistent records, and understand the difference between state incorporation and later steps.

Situations that deserve individualized legal help

Consider a nonprofit attorney when the organization involves:

  • Unusual membership, LLC, trust, chapter, affiliate, joint-venture, or subsidiary structures.
  • A conversion, merger, fiscal sponsorship, or transfer from an existing business or informal group.
  • Schools, healthcare, housing, childcare, international work, significant lobbying, or other regulated activities.
  • Founder or family control, substantial compensation, related-party contracts, loans, leases, or other insider transactions.
  • Restricted gifts, complex fundraising, charitable gaming, commercial activity, or major intellectual-property questions.
  • A rejected filing, IRS request for additional information, threatened dispute, or uncertainty about required clauses.

A tax professional may also be needed for classification, reporting, payroll, sales tax, unrelated business income, or other tax questions. A lawyer and a tax professional may solve different parts of the problem.

A useful middle path: Founders can organize their facts and draft materials first, then pay a qualified attorney to review a focused set of documents and questions. Confirm the engagement scope, price, jurisdiction, and whether filing or follow-up with an agency is included.

What to ask before hiring someone

  1. What is included? Ask whether the engagement covers state formation, bylaws, board action, the IRS application, charity registration, and agency follow-up.
  2. Who submits each filing? Never assume review includes submission or government fees.
  3. What experience applies? Ask about nonprofit and tax-exemption work in the relevant state and with similar activities.
  4. What remains your responsibility? Identify signatures, factual verification, recordkeeping, registrations, and recurring reports.
  5. What happens after filing? Ask whether responding to state or IRS questions is included or billed separately.

Documents and decisions remain connected

Whether founders use a lawyer or prepare materials themselves, the Articles, bylaws, conflict policy, organizational minutes, program description, budget, and IRS application should tell a consistent story. A filing service, template provider, or attorney cannot responsibly invent the organization's mission, programs, finances, and governance facts.

Frequently asked questions

Does the IRS require a lawyer to file Form 1023?

The IRS instructions do not require an attorney to submit Form 1023. An authorized officer, director, trustee, or other official of the organization must digitally sign the application. If the organization wants an eligible representative to represent it before the IRS, it generally submits Form 2848.

Does Illinois require a lawyer to form a nonprofit corporation?

Illinois formation instructions identify who may act as an incorporator and do not state that an attorney must file the Articles. The Secretary of State strongly recommends legal review because additional provisions may be needed for a particular organization.

When should a nonprofit founder hire a lawyer?

Individualized legal help is especially valuable for unusual structures, regulated activities, significant insider transactions, founder compensation, property transfers, fiscal sponsorships, conversions, complex fundraising, or governing-document questions that standard instructions do not resolve.

Can Start Your Cause file nonprofit paperwork for me?

No. Start Your Cause provides customizable templates and educational guidance. It does not submit state or IRS filings, act as a law firm or authorized representative, or provide legal or tax advice.
