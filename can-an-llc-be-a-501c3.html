Entity choice

Can an LLC be a 501(c)(3) nonprofit?

Technically yes—but most individual founders should pause before choosing an LLC. Current IRS standards generally require every member of an LLC seeking its own 501(c)(3) determination to already be a 501(c)(3) organization or a qualifying governmental unit.

Review the LLC requirements →
Practical answer for a first-time founder: If you and other individuals expect to own or serve as members of the entity, a nonprofit corporation will usually align more directly with the standard 501(c)(3) formation path than an LLC under the IRS's current recognition standards.

An LLC can qualify—but not like an ordinary business LLC

The IRS lists limited liability companies among the entity types that may qualify for exemption. That does not mean a typical founder-owned LLC can simply elect to become a charity. The organization must satisfy the general organizational and operational tests for section 501(c)(3), and an LLC must also satisfy the standards in IRS Notice 2021-56.

Those standards are designed to ensure that the LLC remains dedicated to charitable purposes, that its assets cannot be diverted to private owners, and that changes in a member's status do not undermine the exemption.

Current IRS checklist for an LLC seeking recognition

Except where state law limits what may appear in the articles, the IRS standards require both the LLC's articles of organization and operating agreement to include:

  1. Eligible members only. Every member must be a recognized 501(c)(3) organization or a qualifying governmental unit or wholly owned governmental instrumentality.
  2. Express charitable purposes. The documents must limit the LLC to qualifying section 501(c)(3) purposes.
  3. A charitable dissolution provision. Remaining assets must stay dedicated to qualifying exempt purposes.
  4. Private-foundation provisions when applicable. If the LLC will be a private foundation, the required chapter 42 compliance provisions must be included.
  5. A membership contingency plan. The documents must address what happens if a member stops qualifying—for example, suspension of membership rights until status is restored.

The LLC must also represent that these provisions are consistent with applicable state LLC law and are legally enforceable. If state law prohibits additional provisions in the filed articles, Notice 2021-56 describes a limited path for placing the required provisions in the operating agreement instead, provided the articles and operating agreement contain nothing inconsistent.

Why an individually owned LLC is usually the wrong shortcut

An ordinary LLC often gives individual members economic and governance rights. That structure can conflict with the requirement that a 501(c)(3) serve public rather than private interests and permanently dedicate its assets to exempt purposes.

Under the current IRS standards, an individual founder is not one of the permitted member types for an LLC seeking its own favorable 501(c)(3) determination. Creating an LLC first and planning to “convert it later” can therefore create state-law, tax, contract, banking, and asset-transfer questions rather than simplifying formation.

LLCs cannot use Form 1023-EZ

The IRS expressly states that, although an LLC may be eligible to receive recognition under section 501(c)(3), an LLC is not eligible to apply using Form 1023-EZ. An LLC seeking its own exemption must use the full Form 1023 and provide the required organizational documents and LLC-specific information.

Entity structurePotential IRS application route
Eligible nonprofit corporation, trust, or unincorporated associationForm 1023 or, if every official worksheet requirement is satisfied, possibly Form 1023-EZ.
LLC seeking its own 501(c)(3) recognitionFull Form 1023 only. Form 1023-EZ is unavailable.
LLC wholly owned by an exempt parent and treated as disregardedThe IRS says it should not file a separate exemption application merely to obtain its own determination; specialized tax analysis is appropriate.

State formation and federal recognition are separate

A state may permit an LLC to be organized for nonprofit or charitable purposes, but state acceptance does not create federal 501(c)(3) status. Conversely, the federal standards do not replace state entity, registration, reporting, tax, or fundraising requirements.

Before forming any entity, compare the state's nonprofit-corporation law with its LLC law, the proposed ownership or membership structure, the intended funding sources, and the documents funders will expect.

What to decide before filing anything

  • Will any individual person be an owner or LLC member?
  • Does an existing 501(c)(3) or government unit need to own and control the project?
  • Does the project need a separate legal entity at all, or could a fiscal sponsor or existing charity operate it?
  • Will the organization seek public-charity status or be classified as a private foundation?
  • Can the required provisions be placed in enforceable formation documents under state law?
  • Are there existing LLC assets, contracts, employees, licenses, debts, or intellectual property that would complicate a restructure?

Frequently asked questions

Can an LLC qualify for 501(c)(3) status?

Yes, but it must meet the general 501(c)(3) requirements and the IRS's LLC-specific membership, governing-document, charitable-purpose, dissolution, and contingency standards.

Can an LLC owned by individual founders qualify?

An LLC whose members are individual founders generally does not fit the current standards in Notice 2021-56. Those standards require each member to be an existing 501(c)(3) organization or qualifying government unit or instrumentality.

Can an LLC file Form 1023-EZ?

No. An LLC seeking its own recognition must use the full Form 1023.

Does state approval make the LLC federally tax exempt?

No. State entity formation and federal tax-exempt recognition are separate processes.
