501(c)(3) compliance

Can a 501(c)(3) endorse political candidates?

No. A section 501(c)(3) organization may not directly or indirectly participate or intervene in a campaign for or against a candidate for elective public office. The prohibition applies to federal, state, and local candidate campaigns.

Check an activity before publishing →
The simplest rule: Keep the organization's money, accounts, staff time, facilities, events, mailing lists, websites, and social channels out of candidate support or opposition.

What the candidate-campaign prohibition covers

The ban reaches more than a formal endorsement. The IRS identifies direct and indirect campaign intervention, including:

  • Contributing organizational funds to a candidate, campaign, political party, or political action committee.
  • Publishing or distributing statements that support or oppose a candidate.
  • Using the nonprofit's website, email list, social media, facilities, equipment, staff time, or events to favor or oppose a candidate.
  • Distributing campaign materials prepared by someone else.
  • Providing unequal access or a special organizational benefit to a candidate.

The rule applies to candidates for elective public office—not only presidential or congressional candidates. A candidate for local school board, mayor, county office, state legislature, or another elected position is still a candidate for public office.

Can founders and board members speak personally?

Yes, the IRS prohibition is not intended to stop leaders from expressing political views as individuals. The separation must be real. Personal activity should not use nonprofit funds, staff, equipment, mailing lists, branded channels, or events, and the person should not suggest that the organization shares the endorsement.

Using an organizational title for identification can create risk if the context implies the leader is speaking for the nonprofit. A clear statement that the views are personal can help, but a disclaimer does not cure conduct that otherwise uses organizational resources or communicates organizational support.

Can the nonprofit discuss issues during an election?

A charity may continue to advocate its mission and discuss public-policy issues. Risk increases when a communication identifies or depicts a candidate, expresses approval or disapproval of a candidate's positions or actions, appears close to an election, targets voters, or resembles campaign messaging. The IRS evaluates all facts and circumstances.

Avoid assuming that leaving out the words “vote for” makes a message safe. Indirect campaign intervention can still occur when the context shows support or opposition.

Nonpartisan voter education and candidate forums

Genuinely nonpartisan voter registration, get-out-the-vote activity, public forums, and voter education can be permissible. Safeguards commonly include:

  • Using neutral selection rules and offering candidates fair and equal treatment.
  • Covering a broad range of issues rather than topics chosen to advantage one candidate.
  • Using unbiased questions and a neutral moderator.
  • Giving candidates equal opportunities and comparable presentation space.
  • Making no editorial comment, score, ranking, approval, or disapproval.
  • Avoiding coordination with a candidate or campaign.

A program labeled “nonpartisan” can still violate the rule if its actual design, timing, questions, audience, or presentation favors or opposes a candidate.

Lobbying and ballot measures are different

Lobbying generally means attempting to influence legislation. Political campaign intervention means supporting or opposing a candidate for elective office. The IRS treats them as different activities under different rules.

A public charity may conduct some lobbying, although too much can jeopardize exemption. Advocacy for or against a ballot initiative is generally treated as lobbying because voters act as lawmakers on the measure. That does not make every ballot-measure activity unlimited or automatically safe; track lobbying activity and check federal and state registration, disclosure, and campaign-finance rules.

What can happen after a violation?

The IRS states that prohibited campaign intervention may result in denial or revocation of tax-exempt status and the imposition of certain excise taxes. A new organization seeking 501(c)(3) recognition should follow the restriction from the start rather than waiting for a determination letter.

Adopt a written communications and political-activity policy, identify who controls organizational accounts, require prepublication review during election periods, and document how nonpartisan programs were designed.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 501(c)(3) endorse or oppose a candidate?

No. The federal prohibition covers direct or indirect intervention for or against candidates for elective public office.

Can a nonprofit leader endorse a candidate personally?

A leader may speak as an individual, but should not use the nonprofit's resources or imply that the organization shares the endorsement. The facts and circumstances matter.

Can a 501(c)(3) host a candidate forum?

A genuinely nonpartisan forum may be permissible when candidates receive fair and equal treatment, questions cover a broad range of issues, and the organization does not signal approval or disapproval.

Is lobbying the same as supporting a candidate?

No. Lobbying concerns legislation; political campaign intervention concerns candidates. A public charity may conduct some lobbying, but candidate intervention is prohibited.
